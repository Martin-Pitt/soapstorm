# Attack on Entry 06 — Forensics-First — lens: COMPREHENSION

## Walking the officer questions

**Q1 (how did the raid go).** The entry's own walkthrough opens Combat Log → Timeline tab → Front-Distance Lane, i.e. it starts on rung 3 (unbent space), not rung 1 (Ghost Trails, "helicopter view"). But the default tab the floater opens on (section 4.1) is Death Wall, a rung-1 index of individual cases, not the overview. So the very first thing an officer sees when opening the tool for the very first officer question is a grid of case cards with no orientation to time, place, or scale — they must already know to switch tabs and then switch the Overview's radio button from Map-lanes to Front-distance before Q1 gets an answer. That is two mode-switches of friction before the "how did the raid go" question — the headline use case — is even addressed. Worse, the walkthrough jumps from rung 3 straight to rung 1 (Death Wall) and then rung 0 (a Death Report), skipping rung 2 (the parameter sliders) entirely. If rung 2 isn't visited on the flagship "orient to the whole session" question, it isn't actually load-bearing in the ladder — it reads like a rung that exists because the brief asked for one, not because the design needed it there.

**Q3 (why did X die at 14:32).** This one lands well: attribution shares, micro-replay, LOS filmstrip, adjustments, and the Quality panel compose into a genuinely legible single-case answer, and the final prose ("mostly Finn... medium confidence because of Finn's coarser track") is honest and specific. This is the design's strongest moment and the walkthrough proves it end to end.

**Q8 (suspicious behavior).** Also reads well procedurally — Quality-first, then Reaction flag, then explicit refusal to over-claim ("wallhack-consistent hit," never "confirmed cheater"). But it depends on the LOS verdict and reaction-flag numbers being trustworthy, and the attribution/LOS numbers carry a precision problem described below that undercuts exactly the kind of judgment call Q8 exists for.

## Does the officer move the ladder, or just visit a pile of views?

The four rungs are scattered across three different floaters/tabs (Death Wall tab, Timeline/Overview tab, Case File pages) with no shared visual language between them — a session-wide "helicopter view" (rung 1, trails), a slider-driven cluster graph (rung 2, Team Ledger), and an axis-transformed line chart (rung 3, front-distance lane) look and behave nothing alike. Bret Victor's ladder works because the *same picture* is progressively transformed as you climb; here each rung is a distinct custom `LLView` with its own idiom, stitched together only by "click X to open Death Report." That's still useful — cross-linking nouns is good information architecture — but it is not the same thing as a ladder, and the design's own Q1 walkthrough (which never touches rung 2) is evidence the four rungs were assembled to satisfy the brief's taxonomy rather than because climbing through all of them is how an officer actually works.

The more serious version of this: **insights found at one rung don't propagate to the others.** The Team Ledger's confidence-threshold slider is described as recoloring "all in-world avatar markers" live — good — but nothing says it recolors the Death Wall's per-card team fill, or recomputes the Front-Distance Lane's front-line (which is defined by "session-clustering marks as opposing teams," i.e. baked at whatever threshold clustering used, not the officer's currently-dragged value). In the intra-group walkthrough the officer discovers the true split sits near 35%, not the default 60% — but there is no described step where that discovery updates the Death Wall's colors or the Overview's front-line so the whole session re-renders in light of what was just learned. An officer who finds the right threshold on the Ledger and then goes back to the Overview to re-read "how did the raid go" is looking at a stale picture. That is exactly the "stuck on one level" failure Victor warns about, just inverted: the abstraction doesn't carry its own corrections back down to the other abstractions.

## Team drift — the brief's explicit requirement is not actually met

The brief is blunt: *"Team membership can change during a session... present allegiance as an inference with confidence, never a hard fact."* Confidence, yes — the Ledger has per-avatar percentages. But drift over *time* is a different axis than confidence, and nothing in the analysis model or the Team Ledger screen is time-indexed. Label-propagation clustering "exposed with a threshold slider" is described as one session-wide computation; the slider changes the confidence cutoff, not the point in time being asked about. A defector who fights for Ashguard-A for the first hour and Ashguard-B for the second would be assigned one blended (or low-confidence, "splits A/B") label for the whole session, which is worse than useless for exactly the promotion/violation review the brief cites as the primary use case ("was this friendly fire, or had they switched sides by then?"). The Front-Distance Lane does bucket by 2-minute windows for its front-line estimate, so the machinery for windowed team inference clearly exists elsewhere in the same design — it just was not wired into the Team Ledger or the FF flags, which are the two places officers will actually ask "whose side were they on at that moment." This is a hole in the core analysis model, not a screen-layout nitpick.

## Decorative precision in attribution

The Death Report's headline line — "killed by: Finn Drask (72%) | 2nd candidate: Orr Vex (18%) | UNK 10%" — presents a normalized weighted sum (damage share × recency × killing-blow bonus, per section 6) as if it were a calibrated probability. Nothing in the analysis model claims these weights were validated against any ground truth, and two-significant-figure percentages read to a non-technical officer as measured confidence, not "a made-up score that happens to sum to 100." This is the opposite failure from the design's stated principle ("uncertainty as evidence quality, not noise to hide") — here uncertainty is dressed up as more precise than it is. A HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW-style banding (as used everywhere else in the same document, e.g. Reconstruction Confidence) or an explicit "ranking, not a probability" caveat would match the design's own honesty bar; the percentages as shown don't.

## In-world overlay at raid scale

Section 5 describes Ghost Trails as "every avatar's full-session trail" with "death markers persist as small crosses along the trail regardless of cursor position, always clickable," plus LOS fans and confidence-recolored markers, all layered simultaneously. For a 1–2 hour raid with dozens of avatars (the Team Ledger example alone has 11+9+3 = 23, and FFA sims can be larger), that is a lot of persistent geometry to alt-cam around and still read. The document never addresses decluttering: no distance-based fade, no "trails for the selected team/avatar only" default, no LOD as the officer zooms out. The Reconstruction-confidence "desaturate toward grey" idea for low-confidence avatars is a nice touch but actively makes the FFA case worse, not better — a scene of thirty low-confidence FFA combatants would render as a wash of near-grey with a soup of overlapping trails, which is the opposite of the "get a direct, visceral sense" promise rung 0/1 makes. The brief explicitly names alt-cam usability as the primary interaction mode; this needs an answer and doesn't have one.

## Digging and losing the thread

The breadcrumb ("browser-style history") plus a 6-slot pin rail is a reasonable compromise, but two gaps remain. First, a plain linear back/forward history loses branches: dig Death→Avatar→Weapon, go back to Avatar, dig into a different Death, and the first Weapon page falls out of "forward" history with no map of what was visited — for an officer assembling a multi-death violation case this is a real risk of losing track, and the design's own promise ("digging never loses the thread") is asserted, not demonstrated. Second, it's unspecified whether the pin rail and the breadcrumb share one history stack or each pinned tab keeps its own — ambiguous enough that two implementers would build different things.

## What must be kept

- The Death Report as the single hero page with attribution list + bounded micro-replay + LOS filmstrip + Adjustments + Quality stacked together — this is a genuinely good answer to "why did X die," and Q3's walkthrough proves it works end to end.
- The disabled "Show in world" button with a live reason string when overlay gating fails — directly honors the brief's stationary+alt-cam gating and tells the officer *why*, not just *that*, the 3D view is unavailable.
- The consistent refusal to collapse ambiguity into a false single answer (ranked attribution rather than one name; "evidence of a wallhack-consistent hit," never "confirmed cheater"; through-wall suspicion framed as a rate with links to underlying cases and an explicit caveat).
- In-world uncertainty encoding: dashed/thin for track gaps, translucent bands for coarse-quality positions, grey LOS rays when the underlying sample wasn't trustworthy enough to raycast from — this is uncertainty as evidence quality done right, and should survive any redesign.
- Explicit FFA and intra-group-skirmish walkthroughs with concrete fallback behavior (Unclustered bucket, slider-driven re-split) rather than hand-waving that "clustering handles it" — this is more than most designs bother to show and should be kept, once the underlying time-varying-team gap above is fixed.
